**TL;DR:** Biohazard cleanup in Nevada typically costs between $500 and $15,000, depending on the size of the affected area, the type of contamination, and how much material has to be removed. A single-room blood cleanup often falls between $500 and $2,500. An unattended death or large-scale decontamination can run $5,000 to $15,000 or more. Homeowners insurance sometimes covers it, but only under specific conditions.

If you're staring at a scene in your Henderson home right now, cost is probably not your first thought, but it becomes the next question fast, especially once you start talking to an insurance adjuster or a cleanup company. Nevada doesn't have a published state fee schedule for biohazard remediation, so prices are set job by job based on contamination level, square footage, and materials that have to be legally disposed of as medical or biohazardous waste. Here's what actually drives that number, and what Las Vegas Valley homeowners typically pay.

## How much does biohazard cleanup cost in Nevada?

Most biohazard cleanup jobs in Nevada cost between $500 and $15,000, with the bulk of residential jobs landing between $1,500 and $6,000. The range is wide because "biohazard cleanup" covers everything from a small blood spill to a full decomposition scene requiring subfloor removal.

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Single-room blood or bodily fluid cleanup | $500 - $2,500 |
| Unattended death cleanup (early discovery) | $1,500 - $5,000 |
| Unattended death with decomposition / odor | $3,500 - $10,000+ |
| Suicide or traumatic injury scene | $2,000 - $7,500 |
| Sewage or infectious waste contamination | $500 - $3,000 |
| Large-scale or multi-room decontamination | $5,000 - $15,000+ |

These are industry-typical ranges, not a quote. Every scene is different, and a licensed cleanup company should give you a written scope before any work starts, not a number over the phone.

## What drives the price of a biohazard cleanup job?

Five factors move the price more than anything else: how much material has to be removed, how long the contamination sat before discovery, square footage, disposal fees, and whether structural materials (subfloor, drywall, insulation) are affected.

Time is the biggest multiplier. A scene discovered within hours usually stays contained to surface cleaning and disinfection. A scene discovered days later often means porous materials like carpet, padding, and drywall have absorbed fluid and can't be cleaned, only removed and replaced. That shifts the job from a cleaning service into a mini reconstruction project.

Disposal is its own cost line. Biohazardous waste in Nevada has to be bagged, labeled, and transported by a licensed medical waste hauler, not thrown in a regular dumpster. That handling and transport fee is built into most biohazard invoices separately from labor.

Odor is the factor people underestimate. Decomposition odor penetrates deep into subfloor and framing. Neutralizing it sometimes requires sealing and repainting surfaces after cleaning, which adds to both the timeline and the bill. The [IICRC's S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) covers how contaminated materials are assessed for cleanability versus removal, and a credentialed technician should be able to explain which category your scene falls into.

## Will homeowners insurance pay for biohazard cleanup?

Sometimes, but only under specific circumstances tied to how your policy defines a covered loss. Most standard homeowners policies include some coverage for cleanup after an accidental death or trauma, or for damage from a covered peril like a burst pipe itself. But if that pipe backs up and causes sewage contamination, cleanup for the backup is typically covered only if you have a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement. Check your policy wording for how it treats each part of the loss.

What's usually excluded: cleanup tied to criminal activity on the property, cleanup from hoarding conditions, and anything the insurer classifies as gradual contamination rather than a sudden event. Every policy is different, so this is a call to your agent, not an assumption. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) has a plain-English overview of how claims get settled after a covered loss, which is worth reading before that call.

Documentation protects the claim either way. Photos of the scene before cleanup starts, an itemized invoice from the cleanup crew, and a written scope of work are what adjusters ask for. A reputable biohazard company will hand you that paperwork without being asked twice.

## What happens during a professional biohazard cleanup?

A professional biohazard cleanup follows a set sequence: scene assessment, containment, removal of contaminated materials, cleaning and disinfection, and odor control, finishing with a clearance check before the space is returned to you.

1. **Assessment.** A technician evaluates the scope, identifies what's salvageable versus what has to be removed, and documents the scene for insurance purposes.
2. **Containment.** The affected area is sealed off with plastic sheeting to stop cross-contamination into unaffected rooms.
3. **Removal.** Contaminated porous materials (carpet, padding, sometimes subfloor or drywall) are removed and bagged as biohazardous waste.
4. **Cleaning and disinfection.** Remaining surfaces are cleaned with hospital-grade disinfectants following [OSHA's bloodborne pathogen guidelines](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens).
5. **Odor control and final check.** Air scrubbers and sealants address lingering odor, and the technician does a final walkthrough before closing out the job.

This is also the point where crews coordinate directly with the family or property manager on timeline, since most people want the space handled quickly and quietly.
