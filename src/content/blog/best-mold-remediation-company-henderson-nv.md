## What Credentials Should a Mold Remediation Company Have?

The single credential that separates a trained remediator from a cleaning crew is IICRC AMRT, the Applied Microbial Remediation Technician certification. A company holding it has trained technicians who follow a documented protocol rather than guessing.

AMRT training covers how mold grows, how to contain a work area so spores don't spread to clean rooms, and how to verify the job actually worked when it's done. The [Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification](https://www.iicrc.org/) sets this standard nationally, and the work itself should follow the [ANSI/IICRC S520 Standard for Professional Mold Remediation](https://iicrc.org/s520/), which lays out containment levels, PPE requirements, and clearance criteria by the size and severity of the job.

Ask directly: "Is the technician coming to my house AMRT certified, or just the owner?" A lot of companies have one certified person on staff and send uncertified labor to do the actual work. ## Why Does Independent Mold Testing Matter More Than Self-Testing?

A remediation company testing its own finished work has an obvious conflict of interest. Independent, third-party testing from a separate inspector removes that conflict and gives you (and your insurer, if it's a claim) an unbiased result.

The [EPA's guidance on mold testing](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-testing-or-sampling) notes that sampling isn't always necessary to confirm mold is present (visible growth or a musty smell is usually enough to act on), but it is valuable for confirming a job is actually finished. A legitimate remediator will tell you upfront that pre-work testing and post-work clearance testing are separate services, and many will recommend you hire a third-party indoor air quality inspector for the clearance step rather than doing it themselves.

If a company tells you "we'll test it and we'll pass it," that's a flag. If they say "we'll do the remediation, then recommend an independent inspector for clearance," that's the industry-standard way to do it. ## What Does Proper Containment and Clearance Testing Actually Look Like?

Proper mold remediation isolates the affected area with physical barriers before any demolition starts, then runs negative air pressure through a HEPA scrubber so spores get filtered out instead of traveling through your HVAC system into the rest of the house.

That containment is usually 6-mil poly sheeting taped and sealed around the work zone, with a zippered entry flap. A HEPA-filtered air scrubber runs continuously, pulling air through the containment and exhausting it (filtered) outside or into a safe space. Technicians work in full protective gear, which is a sign they're taking exposure seriously, not a sign of alarm.

When the physical removal and cleaning are done, clearance happens: air samples and sometimes surface swabs get compared to a baseline reading, often taken outdoors or in an unaffected room in the same house. A clean clearance result is what tells you, and your insurance adjuster if one's involved, that the job is actually finished rather than cosmetically done. Skipping this step is one of the most common corners cut in the industry.

## What Are Red Flags in a Mold Remediation Quote?

A red-flag quote is vague about scope, skips containment and clearance testing entirely, and pressures you to sign the same day. Any one of these is worth a second opinion before you commit.

Specific things to watch for:

- **No containment mentioned.** If the quote doesn't describe how they'll isolate the work area, ask why.
- **"We'll spray it and it's done."** Surface spraying without removing porous, contaminated material (drywall, insulation, carpet padding) rarely solves the underlying problem.
- **No moisture source identified.** Mold remediation without finding and fixing the leak or humidity source is a repeat job waiting to happen.
- **Vague line-item pricing or a single lump sum with no breakdown.** You should see square footage, labor, equipment days, and disposal separately.
- **No Nevada contractor license number on the paperwork.** Verify any company's license through the [Nevada State Contractors Board](https://www.nvcontractorsboard.com/) before signing anything.
- **High-pressure, same-day-signature tactics.** Mold isn't usually an emergency measured in hours the way a flooded room is; a reputable company gives you time to compare quotes.

## What Questions Should You Ask Before You Sign a Contract?

Before signing, ask who on the crew is AMRT certified, whether containment and HEPA air scrubbing are included, and whether clearance testing is independent or done in-house.

A short list worth asking every company, including us:

1. Is the technician doing the work IICRC AMRT certified, not just the company on paper?
2. What's the containment plan for this specific job?
3. Will you remove porous materials that absorbed moisture, or just surface-clean them?
4. Do you recommend independent clearance testing, and can you work alongside that inspector?
5. What's your Nevada contractor license number, and will you put the scope of work in writing before starting?
6. How will you document the job for my insurance claim if mold resulted from a covered water loss?

If a company answers all six without hesitation and backs it up in writing, you're probably looking at a legitimate remediation protocol, not a quick paint-and-pray job. Homeowners insurance treats mold coverage narrowly, usually only when it traces back to a sudden, covered water event per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), so documentation from day one matters.
