**TL;DR:** HVAC mold remediation in Henderson, NV typically costs $500 to $8,000, depending on whether mold is limited to the condensate pan and drain line or has spread through the ductwork and attic air handler. A simple inspection and air sample runs $300 to $600. Full-system remediation with containment, duct cleaning, and a post-work clearance test can reach $4,000 to $8,000 or more. The number that matters is how far the contamination has traveled before anyone caught it.

If you've noticed a musty smell every time the AC kicks on, or dark speckling around a supply vent in a home anywhere from Green Valley to Anthem, the system is probably growing mold somewhere between the drain pan and the duct runs. Henderson air conditioners run nine or ten months a year, and every one of them produces condensation that has to drain somewhere. When that drain line clogs or a pan sits with standing water, mold has exactly the damp, dark, food-source environment it needs. The cost to fix it depends almost entirely on how early it's caught.

The spread is wide because the scope of work varies enormously: a single contaminated drain pan is a small job, while mold that's colonized ductwork and an attic air handler requires containment, material removal, and coordination with an HVAC contractor.

## What drives HVAC mold remediation cost?

The biggest cost driver is how much of the system is contaminated, not the square footage of the house. A job confined to the drain pan on one air handler costs a fraction of one where spores have traveled through the supply ducts into multiple rooms.

Other factors that move the number:

- **Access.** Henderson air handlers are commonly tucked in attics with limited headroom, which slows down containment and removal work compared to a garage or closet unit.
- **Duct material.** Flexible insulated ductwork that's been colonized usually gets replaced rather than cleaned, since the fibrous liner holds spores. Sheet metal ducts can sometimes be cleaned and treated in place.
- **How long it's been growing.** Mold found within days of the musty smell is cheaper to handle than mold that's had months to spread through insulation and framing near the air handler.
- **Whether the coil or pan needs replacing.** If the evaporator coil or drain pan itself is corroded or degraded, that's an HVAC repair layered on top of remediation, not included in remediation pricing.
- **Containment requirements.** [IICRC S520](https://iicrc.org/s520/) sets the standard for mold remediation work, including containment and negative air pressure for anything beyond a very limited, isolated area.

## Duct cleaning vs. mold remediation: what's the difference?

Duct cleaning removes dust and debris from ductwork; mold remediation removes an active fungal growth and addresses the moisture source feeding it. A duct cleaning service without a moisture fix or antimicrobial treatment will not stop mold from coming back.

This distinction matters because some companies advertise "duct cleaning" for what is actually a mold problem. A legitimate mold job starts with identifying and correcting the moisture source, usually a clogged condensate line, a sagging drain pan, or a leaking plenum, then removes or treats the contaminated material, and finishes with a clearance test. Cleaning alone skips the moisture correction and the verification step. The [EPA's guidance on mold cleanup](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) is clear that fixing the water problem is the first step, before anything else.

## Does homeowners insurance cover mold in HVAC systems in Nevada?

Most standard Nevada homeowners policies cover HVAC mold remediation only when the mold resulted from a sudden, covered water event, such as a burst supply line. Mold caused by a slow condensate leak, a clogged drain line, or ongoing high humidity is typically treated as a maintenance issue and excluded.

Many policies also carry a specific mold coverage cap, often far below the cost of a full remediation, even when the underlying cause is covered. According to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), coverage details vary significantly by carrier and policy language, so it's worth reading the mold exclusion or endorsement section directly before assuming either way. The [Nevada Division of Insurance](https://doi.nv.gov/) is the state resource for questions about a specific policy. Photographing the drain pan, the vent staining, and the air handler before any work begins protects a claim either way, covered or not.

## What does the remediation process actually involve?

A proper HVAC mold remediation job follows a sequence: find the moisture source, contain the work area, remove or treat the contaminated material, dry and disinfect, then verify the air is clear. Skipping any step, especially the fix to the moisture source, means the mold comes back.

1. **Inspection and sampling.** A technician checks the drain pan, coil, and duct interior, and may pull an air or surface sample to confirm the extent of growth.
2. **Source correction.** The clogged drain line gets cleared, the pan gets leveled or replaced, or whatever is keeping the system wet gets fixed first.
3. **Containment.** Plastic sheeting isolates the work area and the HVAC system is shut down to prevent spreading spores through the house during removal.
4. **Removal and treatment.** Contaminated duct sections, insulation, or filter media are removed; non-porous surfaces like the coil and pan are cleaned and treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial.
5. **Clearance testing.** A follow-up air sample confirms spore counts in the home are back to a normal background level before containment comes down.
