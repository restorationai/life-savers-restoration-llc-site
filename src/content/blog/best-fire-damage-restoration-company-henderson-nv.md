## What should you ask a Henderson fire restoration company before you sign anything?

Ask to see their Nevada contractor's license number, proof of insurance, and a written scope of work before you sign a contract, not after. Any legitimate company hands these over without hesitation.

Nevada requires contractors performing restoration and rebuild work to be licensed through the [Nevada State Contractors Board](https://www.nvcontractorsboard.com/), and you can verify a license number yourself before a crew ever sets foot on your property. The board also publishes a [consumer checklist](https://www.nvcontractorsboard.com/resources/consumer-checklist/) that covers exactly this kind of hire. Life Savers Restoration LLC carries Nevada license #0091001 and is an IICRC Certified Firm with FSRT (Fire & Smoke Restoration) training on staff, certifications you can ask any company to name specifically, not just "we're certified."

Beyond licensing, ask for a written scope that itemizes what's being cleaned, what's being removed, and what's being rebuilt, with square footage and material types noted. A verbal estimate scrawled on a business card is not a scope. If a company won't put the job in writing before asking for a signature, that's your answer.

## Should mitigation and rebuild be the same company, or two different ones?

One company handling both mitigation and rebuild usually means fewer handoffs, fewer repeated inspections, and one point of contact for your insurance adjuster instead of two competing scopes. It's not required, but it simplifies a process that's already stressful.

Mitigation is the emergency phase: boarding up windows, extracting water used to fight the fire, running [IICRC S700](https://iicrc.org/s700/)-standard cleaning on soot and smoke residue, and stabilizing the structure so it doesn't get worse. Rebuild is the construction phase: replacing drywall, flooring, cabinetry, and anything that had to be demolished. When the same company does both, the rebuild crew already knows exactly what was removed and why, because they documented it during mitigation.

When two separate companies split the job, you're the one relaying information between them, and discrepancies between their scopes become your problem to resolve, usually under pressure from an insurance timeline. If you do split the work, get both scopes in writing and have each company acknowledge the other's documentation before demolition starts.

## How do you know smoke odor is actually gone, not just masked?

Real odor removal treats the source materials, HVAC system, and contents separately, not just the room air. A company spraying air freshener or running a fogger once and calling it done hasn't solved the problem.

Smoke odor hides in three places people forget: inside HVAC ductwork, in porous wall cavities behind drywall that looks fine, and absorbed into soft contents like upholstery, drapes, and clothing. A thorough job includes duct cleaning or sealing where soot has settled inside the system, thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment matched to the severity of the smoke, and a separate contents-cleaning process for items that can be salvaged versus items that can't. Charring that's penetrated wood framing or subfloor usually means that material gets removed, not deodorized, because sealing odor into a structural member doesn't actually remove it.

If a company's plan for odor is a single spray-and-go visit with no mention of ductwork or contents, ask what happens if the smell comes back in two weeks. It usually does.

## How does a fire restoration company work with your insurance adjuster?

A company experienced with Henderson fire claims documents damage with photos and a written scope before demolition, then communicates that scope directly to your adjuster so the claim moves on the facts, not on guesswork. You should never feel like you're the go-between.

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental fire damage, including smoke and soot cleanup tied to the fire, though coverage details and limits vary by policy and insurer (see the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to settling claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster)). Keeping your own copy of every photo, invoice, and scope document protects you if there's ever a dispute about what was damaged versus what was pre-existing. A company that documents thoroughly from day one, before tearing anything out, is doing you a favor whether or not you ever need to lean on it.

## What are the red flags of storm-chaser pricing and pressure tactics?

The biggest red flag is pressure to sign a contract on the spot, before you've seen a written scope or had time to call your insurer. Legitimate companies expect you to take a day to think it over.

Other signs: a company that showed up uninvited within hours of the fire soliciting work, a quote with no line-item breakdown, demands for large upfront cash payment before any work begins, or a contract that signs over your insurance claim rights (an "assignment of benefits") without explaining what that means. None of these automatically mean fraud, but together they're worth slowing down for.
