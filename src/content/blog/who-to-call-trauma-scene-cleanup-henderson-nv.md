## Who do you call first: police or a cleanup company?

Call the police or 911 first if you have not already. A trauma cleanup company cannot legally begin work at a scene the Henderson Police Department or the Clark County Coroner has not yet released.

Once officers have finished their investigation (photographs, evidence collection, and in a death case, the coroner's work), they will tell you the scene is released. That release, verbal or written, is the point where a biohazard cleanup crew can be scheduled. If you are not sure whether the scene has been officially released, ask the responding officer directly before anyone goes back inside.

## What happens between scene release and cleanup starting?

Between release and cleanup, the property owner typically needs to notify a landlord or HOA, secure the space, and make one phone call to a cleanup company. Nothing inside the affected room should be touched, moved, or cleaned during this window.

If the property is a rental, most Nevada leases require notifying the landlord promptly after an incident like this, even before cleanup is scheduled. If the home is in an HOA community, a quick notice to management can head off questions about contractor vehicles on the street. Keep the room closed off; a sealed door limits odor spread and keeps anyone from walking into an area that still carries biohazard risk.

## Why shouldn't family members clean this themselves?

Blood and other bodily fluids can carry bloodborne pathogens such as hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV, which is why [OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens) exists to govern how this material is handled, even outside a traditional workplace. A mop and household cleaner do not neutralize that risk.

Unattended death cleanup carries an added complication most families do not anticipate: fluids can soak into subfloor, baseboards, and framing well past what is visible on the surface flooring. A trained crew tests moisture and odor penetration before deciding what gets treated versus removed, which is not something a rental steam cleaner or a weekend of scrubbing can replicate. For the fuller explanation of what this type of job involves, see [our general guide to trauma scene cleanup](/blog/trauma-scene/).

## What happens when you call a trauma cleanup company?

The first call is mostly about logistics: your address, whether the scene has been released, and a general description of the affected area so the right equipment gets loaded before the crew leaves the shop. You do not need to describe anything in detail, and a competent dispatcher will not ask you to.

On arrival, the crew suits up in sealed protective equipment before entering, sets up containment to keep the affected area isolated from the rest of the home, and begins assessment of what needs to be removed versus treated. If odor has settled into the structure, that gets addressed as part of the scope rather than left for later.

## Does homeowners or renters insurance pay for trauma cleanup?

Some homeowners and renters policies include coverage for biohazard or trauma cleanup after a death, suicide, or violent crime, but this varies significantly by insurer and policy language, so check your specific policy before assuming either way. A [standard homeowners policy](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is built around sudden, accidental loss, and many insurers treat trauma cleanup as a covered peril under that framework, though not all do.

In cases involving a crime, Nevada's Victims of Crime Compensation Program is sometimes a separate avenue worth asking about, independent of a homeowner's policy. A written scope and photo documentation from the cleanup company helps either path move faster. No company can promise what your insurer will approve, so ask for that documentation up front.

## What questions should you ask before a crew arrives?

Before scheduling, it is reasonable to ask a few direct questions: is the crew IICRC certified, do they carry their own biohazard disposal permits, will they provide a written scope before starting, and do they handle odor and subfloor work if fluids have penetrated past the surface flooring. A company that answers these plainly, without hedging, is one worth trusting with a job this personal.
