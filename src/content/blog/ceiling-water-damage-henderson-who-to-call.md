If you just noticed a brown ring spreading across your bedroom ceiling, or a soft spot near the bathroom that gives when you press it, you're probably standing there wondering whether this is a five-minute fix or a five-figure problem. In Henderson, the honest answer is: it depends on where the water is coming from, and that's not always obvious from underneath the drywall. A stain over the bathroom could be a slow supply line leak. A stain near an exterior wall after a monsoon storm could be a roof flashing failure. A drip from a hallway ceiling under an AC closet is often a clogged condensate line. Each of those needs a different first call.

## Is a ceiling water stain always an emergency?

A ceiling stain is an emergency the moment you see active dripping, sagging drywall, or a bulge that's growing, because those signs mean water is pooling above your head and the ceiling could fail. A dry, old-looking tan stain that hasn't changed size in months is a lower-priority repair, but it still means the moisture source was never fixed properly.

Active water changes the math fast. Saturated drywall loses most of its structural strength within hours, and a sagging section can drop without warning. If you see water actively coming through, place a bucket, move anything valuable out from underneath, and shut off power to any light fixture or fan in that ceiling section at the breaker before you do anything else.

## What's usually causing ceiling water damage in a Henderson home?

In Henderson, the three most common sources are an overflowing AC condensate drain pan, a leaking upstairs bathroom, and a roof or flashing failure during monsoon season. Each leaves a different pattern and points to a different first call.

An AC drain pan overflow shows up as a stain directly under or near an attic air handler or a closet HVAC unit, often worse during the hottest months when the system runs constantly and the condensate line clogs with algae. An upstairs bathroom leak usually appears below a tub, shower pan, or toilet flange, and gets worse with use rather than with weather. A roof leak tends to follow Mojave monsoon storms in July and August, when wind-driven rain finds a gap in flashing around a vent pipe or chimney that stayed bone-dry the rest of the year. Homes in neighborhoods like Green Valley and Anthem with low-slope tile roofs are particularly prone to flashing failures that only show themselves during a hard monsoon downpour.

## Should you call a plumber, a roofer, or a restoration company first?

Call a restoration company first whenever you don't already know the exact source, because they'll find the cause, stop ongoing damage, and bring in the right trade if one is needed. Call a plumber directly only if you've already confirmed a supply line or drain failure, and call a roofer directly only if you've confirmed a roof or flashing leak.

The reason the restoration call usually comes first is diagnostic. A plumber checks plumbing. A roofer checks the roof. Neither one is equipped to trace moisture through drywall, insulation, and framing to confirm which system actually failed, and guessing wrong means paying for a service call that didn't fix anything. A restoration technician uses a moisture meter and often a thermal camera to map exactly where the water traveled before anyone cuts into the ceiling. ## What happens once a restoration crew is on site?

A restoration crew checks the moisture content of the drywall, the insulation above it, and the framing before anything gets cut open, using a penetrating moisture meter and often a borescope through a small inspection hole. This tells them whether the material can be dried in place or has to come out.

Wet blown-in or batt insulation almost never dries in place and usually gets removed, since trapped moisture against the drywall just keeps feeding the problem. Framing (the ceiling joists) gets tested too; wood holding above roughly 17-19% moisture content typically needs active drying with air movers and a dehumidifier before any repair work starts, following the drying principles in the [IICRC S500 water damage standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). If the water sat long enough to be a Category 2 or 3 loss, meaning it carried contamination rather than coming from a clean supply line, affected insulation and drywall are removed rather than dried, and the area gets treated before rebuild.

## When does the ceiling actually have to come down?

A ceiling section has to come down when the drywall has sagged, delaminated, or stayed wet long enough that it won't hold its integrity, or when the insulation above it is saturated and can't be dried in place. A small, caught-early drip stain sometimes dries and gets repainted without demo.

Before anything is cut or removed, take photos and a short video of the stain, the sag, and any visible dripping from multiple angles, with a date-stamped phone photo if possible. Most homeowners insurance policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a pipe that burst overnight, but the [documentation has to show the damage before mitigation started](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) to support the claim. Gradual leaks that went unaddressed for a long time are frequently denied, so this is also the moment to flag anything that looks like it's been there a while. If the exposed area reveals staining consistent with mold growth, which can start within 24 to 48 hours on wet drywall per the EPA, that gets addressed separately through [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) rather than just a drywall patch.
