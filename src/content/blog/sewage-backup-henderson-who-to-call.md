## What does sewage backing up into a floor drain, tub, or toilet mean?

It almost always means the main sewer line or a branch line is blocked downstream of your fixtures, forcing wastewater back up through the lowest drain in the system. That's usually a floor drain, a basement tub, or the lowest-level toilet.

Common causes in Henderson homes include tree roots intruding into older clay or cast-iron laterals, grease and wipes buildup in the line, or a collapsed section of pipe. Because the water has traveled through the sewer system, it carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites regardless of how clean it looks when it surfaces. This is one reason [Category 3 water damage](/blog/category-3-water-damage/) is treated differently from a clean supply-line leak.

## Who do you call first: a plumber or a restoration company?

Call a licensed plumber first if the line is still actively backing up or clogged, since nothing dries or gets cleaned properly until the source is stopped. Call a restoration company as soon as the flow stops (or in parallel, if you have two numbers to dial) to begin extraction, removal of contaminated materials, and disinfection.

These are two different trades solving two different problems. A plumber's job is mechanical: clearing the line with a snake or hydro-jet, running a camera inspection, and repairing or replacing the pipe if it's damaged. A restoration crew's job is the contamination: extracting standing water, removing porous materials that can't be saved, and treating remaining surfaces with an EPA-registered antimicrobial. Skipping the second step because the water "looks gone" is how Henderson homes end up with lingering odor and hidden bacterial contamination weeks later.

## What happens once you call a restoration company?

Expect a short intake call about the source, the affected rooms, and whether anyone in the home has health concerns, followed by a technician who isolates the contaminated area before touching anything else. Have your insurance policy number handy if you have one, along with photos of the standing water and any items already affected.

On arrival, a trained technician will typically:

1. Confirm the flow has stopped and the area is safe to enter
2. Set up containment to keep contamination from spreading to unaffected rooms
3. Extract standing water with equipment rated for black water
4. Remove and bag contaminated porous materials for disposal
5. Apply an antimicrobial treatment to remaining structural surfaces
6. Set drying equipment and monitor moisture levels until materials reach a safe level

None of this should involve a shop vac, a mop, or open fans pushed into other rooms, all of which spread contamination rather than contain it.

## What should you keep kids and pets away from?

Keep children, pets, and anyone who isn't wearing protective gear out of any room touched by the backup until a professional has cleaned and disinfected it. Category 3 water can contain E. coli, salmonella, and other pathogens that pose a real risk through skin contact or inhalation of aerosolized particles.

Close the door to the affected room if you can, and don't let anyone walk contaminated water onto carpet or flooring in other parts of the house. The [CDC's guidance on cleaning safely after a disaster](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/safety/index.html) recommends gloves, boots, and avoiding direct contact with sewage-contaminated water entirely, which is one reason this type of loss gets handed to trained technicians rather than handled with household cleaning supplies.

## What gets thrown out versus saved after a sewage backup?

Most porous materials that touched Category 3 water get discarded rather than cleaned, because contamination can soak deep into the material's structure where disinfectant can't fully reach. That typically includes carpet, carpet pad, drywall up to several inches above the waterline, and insulation.

Non-porous and semi-porous materials, like sealed concrete, tile, and some solid-wood furniture, can often be cleaned, disinfected, and dried rather than replaced. A technician will test and document each affected material before deciding, which also creates the paper trail an insurance adjuster will want to see.

## Does homeowners insurance cover sewage backup in Nevada?

Most standard homeowners policies do not automatically cover sewer or drain backup. Coverage usually requires a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement added to the policy, so the real answer depends on what's actually written into your specific policy.

Check your declarations page for a "water backup," "sewer backup," or "sump overflow" endorsement before assuming you're covered or denied. The [Insurance Information Institute explains which disasters standard homeowners policies cover](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) and where backup coverage typically falls outside the base policy. If you're unsure what your Nevada policy includes, the [Nevada Division of Insurance](https://doi.nv.gov/) can help you understand your options, and documenting the loss with photos and an itemized scope of work from your restoration company strengthens any claim either way.
